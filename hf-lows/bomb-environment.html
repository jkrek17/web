Environment check: is this low set up for rapid growth?

← Back to the archive
ERA5 proxy, not direct observation. ERA5 is a weather model re-run over past years with observations blended in. Everything here is built on ERA5 and on storm tracks found in ERA5 itself (pipeline A), seasons 2004-05 to 2025-26. The one exception is the GFS gust rule section, which scores GFS forecasts (2021-22 to 2025-26) against OPC’s archive. The “gale”, “storm” and “hurricane force” classes are cut points on ERA5’s gust index, the strongest ocean gust within about 430 nm of the low (38, 54 and 71.7 kt). Only the 71.7 kt cut is tied to the archive; the other two are rough labels, not calibrated on observed storms. Each season’s probabilities come from a model that had not seen that season, and they are model output, not observations. Research, not a forecast product.
What this page shows

For one storm at a time, it shows whether the upper-level setup (a trough aloft over strong low-level temperature contrast) is one in which lows have deepened rapidly, and the model’s odds of gale, storm or hurricane-force gusts in the next 24 hours. Use it to see whether a storm you are watching sits in the favourable corner and how that has changed through its life.

  • What is it? For one storm at a time: which of four upper-level regions it sits in (forcing from a trough aloft against low-level temperature contrast, called baroclinicity), where it sits on two further diagrams (jet strength against vorticity advection, and jet strength against baroclinicity), and the model’s chance that its strongest gust over the next 24 hours is below gale, gale, storm or hurricane force.
  • The four regions. Quiet, baroclinic only (fuel but little trigger), forcing only (trigger but little fuel) and coupled (both). Coupled lows are 19 % of all positions but hold of the rapid deepening: both ingredients together matter far more than either alone. The dividing lines are simply the upper third of each variable, not physical thresholds.
  • How to read the diagrams. Each box is a slice of all 159,430 low positions in 2004-05 to 2025-26 at 00 and 12 UTC. Darker means a larger share of lows in that slice deepened rapidly over the next 24 hours (at least 1 Bergeron: 24 hPa in 24 h at 60°N, the classic “bomb”). The storm’s path runs through the boxes as it evolves.
  • Does it work?
  • Reading a GFS forecast gust. The GFS gust rule below turns a forecast peak gust into odds that OPC calls the low hurricane force, with the 72 kt and 64 kt cut-offs side by side. No gust level is a sure thing.

Regions

Which kind of setting is this low in?

These are names for boxes on a chart of two ERA5 quantities, not proven mechanisms. Vorticity advection at 500 hPa is one measure of upper-level forcing, and the Eady growth rate (how favourable the temperature contrast and wind shear are for growth) is one measure of low-level baroclinicity, so “forcing only” means weak baroclinicity by this measure, not none. A storm often moves between regions within a day (the strip under the outlook below), so one fix is a snapshot, not a type. The cut points were set at the upper third of each variable after the first run and describe the data; they were not planned in advance. Shares combine all positions, ERA5 proxy, pipeline A. The gust columns pool storms that were already deep with ones that were not; the outlook model above, which knows the storm’s own state, is the sharper wind estimate. Only the 71.7 kt cut is tied to the archive.

Diagrams

Where the storm sits, and the path it took

Box size is equal in rank, not in units: each axis is cut at its sixth-percentiles over all lows, so the boxes hold about the same number of fixes. Numbers in the boxes are the rapid-deepening share (%). Dot = a 00 or 12 UTC fix; the ringed dot is the selected one; the line runs from the first fix to the last. A line that climbs toward the dark corner is a storm entering a favourable setting.

Outlook through the storm’s life

Each column is the outlook made at that fix for the following 24 hours. The coloured strip under it shows which of the four regions the low was in at that fix. The line is the gust index ERA5 actually reached at that fix (kt, right scale), with dashed lines at 38, 54 and 71.7 kt. The outlook at one fix is compared with the line 12 and 24 hours later.

GFS gust rule

When the GFS forecast gust reaches about 72 kt, how often is OPC calling hurricane force?

This is the raw GFS forecast gust, with no model on top: for each forecast low, the highest ocean gust within 800 km, set against whether OPC’s archive had a hurricane-force fix near the same place and time. Five seasons (2021-22 to 2025-26), October to April, forecasts from 00 and 12 UTC, 0 to 48 hours ahead; 90,131 forecast fixes. The cut-off that matches OPC’s hurricane-force counts is about 72 kt (71.5 to 72.5 kt depending on the season held out).

Read it as odds, not a switch. A bigger gust does not make OPC’s call certain: the share peaks near two in three and does not reach 100%. No gust band is a sure thing.

OPC is not a measurement. Its call is an analyst’s judgement of all the data available, and can lean towards caution for customers, so it can sit above or below the wind the model shows. A GFS gust with no OPC call is not necessarily a GFS error, and a missed OPC call may be a GFS under-forecast or an OPC over-call. This table cannot tell which: that needs observed winds (scatterometer, ships, buoys). “OPC agreed” means OPC also called it, not that the forecast was correct.

At about 72 kt the forecast catches roughly half of OPC’s hurricane-force lows and OPC agrees with about half of its calls. Lowering the cut to 64 kt catches about nine in ten but OPC agrees with only about three in ten of the calls. ERA5 proxy lows are not used here: these are GFS forecast fixes found by the same tracker, scored against OPC’s archive. Five seasons; fixes within one storm are not independent, so no intervals are shown. The “72 kt” cut-off was matched to OPC counts on these same seasons, one season held out at a time. Why the share stops rising above about 85 kt is not known.

Does it work

Skill, tested one season at a time

Scores are measured against simply quoting the usual frequency for that basin and month (0 is no better, 1 is perfect). Each of the 22 seasons (2004-05 to 2025-26) was left out in turn and scored by a model fitted on the other 21; ERA5 proxy, pipeline A. RPSS (ranked probability skill score) scores the four-class outlook; BSS (Brier skill score) is for rapid deepening. “State only” knows the storm’s own depth, deepening so far, speed, current gust index, latitude and season. The other columns add environment terms.

The environment adds a gain that is clear but small beside the storm’s own state, which carries most of the skill. The two diagrams show the part of the environment that matters most visibly; the full model uses 13 environment terms plus the Hart cyclone-phase terms and does better than either diagram alone. The shading in a box is a share across all lows in that box, mixing storms that were already deep with ones that were not; it is a guide, not a forecast for one storm.

Limits

What this does not show

  • ERA5 is a reanalysis. Its gusts read low in the strongest storms and have not been checked against independent observations here.
  • Gale (38 kt) and storm (54 kt) are nominal cuts on the gust index. The archive lists hurricane-force lows only, so they cannot be calibrated on observed events.
  • The outcome is sampled at the next two 00 and 12 UTC fixes, so a gust peak between samples is missed.
  • A track that ends within 24 hours (filled or merged) is scored on the fixes it has, so such storms are counted as below gale.
  • The model was fitted on ERA5 and every score above uses ERA5 for both the inputs and the outcome, so it is a best case for real forecasts. A separate test fed frozen versions of it GFS forecasts for five seasons (2021-22 to 2025-26). At 24 h lead the full model scored +0.351 against climatology on GFS (+0.386 when fed ERA5, so GFS costs about 0.035) and the state-only version +0.315 (+0.343 on ERA5). The environment terms kept about 85 % of their ERA5 gain on GFS (jet and trough 77 %, jet and baroclinicity 93 %). Five seasons only, GFS gusts, skin temperature and heat fluxes are the model’s own rather than ERA5’s, and ERA5 is still the truth there, so this is a check on the inputs, not on observed storms.
  • Storms listed here are the ones the Events tab can show on the map: OPC’s hurricane-force storms with a matched pipeline A track, and the proxy storms OPC never listed.
