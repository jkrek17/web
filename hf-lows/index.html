Reading it
Each card is one chart of the events the filters above select: events per season, the share that deepen explosively (“bomb” storms), the calendar-month cycle, lowest central pressure, time at hurricane force, fastest 24-hour deepening, events with no analyzed centre (mostly Greenland tip jets), and peak intensity by latitude. The cards are independent of each other.
Data
OPC’s own archive of hurricane-force lows, kept by hand, in June–May seasons. The basin, season, month and pressure filters above apply.
Caution
These count archive events, not all storms. The first seasons are short-counted, not quiet. Analysts began logging developing-stage positions in 2013–14 (Pacific) and 2017–18 (Atlantic), which lengthens tracks without changing the weather: see . Events: how the project’s ERA5 storm model rated each OPC hurricane-force storm before OPC called it, then the full list of archive events. Use it to see how much warning the model gave and which storms it missed. ERA5 proxy, not direct observation.

Reading it
The top table, switched by the menu, shows either each OPC storm with the highest model chance of hurricane force (within 24 hours) before OPC’s first hurricane-force fix, and how many hours ahead the chance first reached 50% (0.5), or ERA5 storms the model rated highly that OPC never listed. Each row has a link to show that storm on the map. The lower table lists every archive event the filters select; the button downloads it as CSV.
Data
OPC archive events from 2004–05 on for the model comparison, matched to ERA5 storm tracks (pipeline A) within about 270 nm of OPC’s peak; storms with no matching track are counted but not scored.
Caution
The comparison is descriptive, not a forecast verification, and the model is run on ERA5, a stand-in for observations. A storm the model never rated is often one ERA5 never took to hurricane force. An ERA5 storm absent from OPC’s list is not evidence that OPC missed it: that would need scatterometer, ship or buoy winds. “Recorded” columns in the lower table change with logging practice; “HF” columns compare across seasons.

Data
ERA5 reanalysis (a weather model re-run over past years with observations blended in), pipeline A storm tracks, 1979–2025. The pressure and depth lists run across the whole period; the gust list is ranked within 1979–2000 and within 2004–2025 only.
Caution
ERA5 reads low in the most intense storms and fewer observations went into it in the 1980s, so a 1980s storm and a 2010s storm are not directly comparable, and the gust ranks never cross 2001. Tropical-cyclone-linked storms are included and flagged. Storm names are inferred from date and position, not recorded in the data.

What this shows

Teleconnections: whether a large-scale climate state (ENSO, NAO, PNA, AO, MJO) goes with a different pattern of hurricane-force lows. In short: mostly no clear link was found, and the page shows how large an effect could have been seen.

Reading it
Start with the table of standard comparisons: a marked cell means part of the storm track differs from the usual by more than chance. The composite map shows where storm density differs; the regression uses the index as a number and asks whether it shifts the storms, and how large an effect this archive could have detected. A null result is a result, not a missing one.

Caution

This tab is the technical reference: what the OPC archive holds, how it was recorded, how each number on the other tabs is worked out, and what to be careful about. If you read only one part, read “What is in the archive” and “A change in recording practice”.

What is in the archive

Each event is an extratropical low tracked at 6-hourly synoptic times while it was at, or developing toward, hurricane force. Tracking generally starts at or shortly before the low reaches that intensity and ends when it weakens below storm force, is absorbed, or moves out of the forecast area — so these are not full cyclone life cycles, and the archive says nothing about lows that never reached hurricane force.

A change in recording practice

Partway through the record, analysts began logging developing-hurricane-force (DHF) fixes before each storm’s first hurricane-force fix, and storm-force (S) fixes after its last. This began with the 2013–14 Pacific season and the 2017–18 Atlantic season. It makes each event’s recorded track longer without making the storm any different, and any statistic measured over the whole recorded track moves with it.

Category codes

Events with no analyzed central pressure

A little over 2 % of Atlantic fixes carry NOPRES instead of a pressure, and their median position is 60.0°N, 42.1°W — Cape Farewell, the southern tip of Greenland. These are overwhelmingly tip jet and barrier jet events: the flow accelerates around and along the Greenland terrain and produces hurricane force winds with no closed low centre to analyze. They peak in December–February, run short (a median of two fixes), and are frequent enough — a few per season, more in recent ones — that ignoring them would misstate the climatology.

Because a missing pressure here means “there is no centre” rather than “the centre was not measured”, these events are given their own class instead of being mixed into pressure statistics:

The classification needs both conditions. Location alone is not diagnostic: 185 ordinary pressure-bearing lows also track through the same region. Treat the tip jet label as a well-supported candidate flag, not a verified classification — confirming one takes the surface analysis and a wind field, which this archive does not carry.

Every pressure-based figure on this page — the median, the distribution, the deepening rates — is computed over events with an analyzed centre. The event counts, seasonal cycle and maps include every class, so counts and pressure statistics deliberately describe slightly different populations. Use the Event type filter to isolate either one.

Derived quantities

Minimum central pressure
The lowest analyzed pressure over the tracked fixes. It is the minimum within the tracked window, which may not be the cyclone's true lifetime minimum.
Time at hurricane force
Count of hurricane force fixes × 6 h. With 6-hourly analyses the true duration is only resolved to ±6 h, and a low that peaked between synoptic times can be missed entirely.
Maximum 24-hour deepening (Bergerons)
The largest pressure fall over any 18–24 h window between hurricane-force fixes, scaled to 24 h and normalized by latitude (the version over every recorded fix is kept alongside it; see A change in recording practice): B = (Δp / 24 hPa) × sin 60° / sin φ, following Sanders and Gyakum (1980). B ≥ 1 is the classic “bomb” threshold. Most hurricane-force periods last less than 18 h, so this can be measured for only about a third of events — and those are biased toward the longer-lived, more intense ones. The headline explosive share is taken over all events, counting those that cannot be measured as not explosive, so it is a lower bound rather than a property of the measurable minority.
Translation speed
Great-circle distance between consecutive fixes divided by elapsed time, in knots; reported as the mean from the first to the last hurricane-force fix, with the whole-track figure kept alongside.
Season
June–May, labelled by the starting year: an event on 2 January 2015 belongs to season 2014–15. This keeps a single cool season in one bucket.

Known limitations

  • The default view starts at the 2004–05 season. Pacific entries begin in February 2002 and Atlantic in September 2003, so earlier seasons are partial; they are still in the data and can be selected, but they are marked provisional and are excluded from the default period of record.
  • Positions and pressures are analyst estimates recorded to the nearest tenth of a degree and whole hPa; no reanalysis has been applied.
  • Counts per season reflect both real variability and changes in analysis practice over 20+ years. A trend here is not necessarily a climate signal. The clearest case is described under A change in recording practice, above: durations, track lengths and deepening measured over the whole recorded track are not comparable across 2013 (Pacific) or 2017 (Atlantic).
  • Where one archive ID covered two unrelated lows, the build split it into lettered events (…a, …b) — noted in that event's detail drawer.
  • Pressures outside 880–1060 hPa and non-numeric sentinels (NOPRES, XXX, NA) are treated as “not analyzed”, not as zero.

Ocean currents layer

This archive’s fix-density and pressure-ramp maxima sit almost exactly over the Gulf Stream and the Kuroshio. That is not decoration: the sharp sea-surface-temperature gradient across those western boundary currents supplies the low-level baroclinicity and surface heat flux that feeds explosive cyclogenesis. The optional Ocean currents layer on the Map tab (off by default) draws a multi-year mean surface current field underneath the tracks, fix density, or point layers so that relationship can be checked by eye — put it under Fix density and see whether the storm track belt actually lines up with the current, rather than assuming it does. Co-location shown this way is not proof of causation, and the field is a historical mean (see Credits), not the conditions during any specific event.

Rebuilding

Export each basin tab of the source workbook to data/hf_lows/ and run python3 tools/build_hf_lows.py. The script regenerates everything under docs/data/; committing the result publishes the GitHub Pages preview. The production copy on the NOAA web server is separate: it is updated with tools/publish.py, which does not go through GitHub at all. See the root README.md for both.
