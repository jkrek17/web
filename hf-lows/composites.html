Composites: hurricane force lows against storm-force-only lows

← Back to the archive
Averages from ERA5, not observations. ERA5 is a reanalysis: a weather model re-run over past years and blended with the observations available. Every figure here is built from ERA5 fields around storm tracks that the project found in ERA5 itself (“pipeline A”), for the 2004-05 to 2025-26 seasons. An “HF low” here is a storm whose ERA5 wind field looks like the ones OPC warns on as hurricane force: the strongest ocean gust within about 430 nm reaches 71.7 kt. That is a stand-in (a proxy) for OPC’s sustained-wind warning, it has not been validated before the archive begins, and ERA5 reads low in the strongest storms. The archive on the other tabs is the record; this page is research.

What this page shows

Averages of the weather map around hurricane-force (HF) lows, compared with lows that only reached storm force. A forecaster can use it to see what the typical setup looks like: where the upper trough and jet sit, how steep the pressure gradient gets, and how early a storm looks different. It is a picture of the average, not a way to pick out the next HF low.

  • What does the typical setup look like? In both basins an upper trough (500 hPa heights well below normal) and a strong jet sit over a deepening low. In the Atlantic the average low at onset is 992 hPa near Iceland, with the trough 80 m below normal over it and a jet of 48 m/s (about 93 kt) along its south side. In the Pacific the same pattern is weaker (the trough is 29 m below normal) because HF lows start all across the basin. Basin maps below.
  • What comes before an HF low? A slightly deeper upper trough and a stronger jet than before a low that only reaches storm force. In the Atlantic box (53°N, 41°W), 500 hPa heights are 7 m lower and the jet 1.1 m/s stronger over days −10 to −4 (day 0 is the onset), and 22 m lower and 1.9 m/s stronger on the onset day. The Pacific is weaker (6 m, 0.8 m/s). Sea-surface temperature does not differ.
  • How does an HF low differ from a storm-force low? It is deeper (971 against 984 hPa in the Atlantic, 974 against 985 in the Pacific), with a pressure gradient about 60% steeper, the strongest gust closer to the centre (about 163 against 220 nm in the Atlantic, about 126 against 180 nm in the Pacific) and a wider area of 48-kt gusts. At equal central pressure the wider wind area survives in the Atlantic (about 21 nm wider) and not in the Pacific.
  • When do they start to differ? The pressure gradient separates first, 36 to 48 hours before the peak; central pressure 12 to 24 hours before. The larger hemispheric pattern changes how HF lows look only weakly (Atlantic only, and not robust). Among deepening storms, the larger-scale environment separates those that reach HF, but those storms are already deeper and older.
  • What does the air around an HF low look like? Averaged over every HF low (storm-centred maps below), the 500 hPa low starts about 1,080 nm to the left of the track two days before onset. It is over the storm by onset in the Atlantic (by the peak in the Pacific). The jet maximum is behind the storm by then (and to its right in the Atlantic). Upper-level divergence sits just ahead of the surface low. Part of this wind pattern is the storm’s own circulation.

“Below normal” throughout means below the 1990–2019 average for the same day and hour (an anomaly).

Typical pattern

The typical weather pattern for an HF low, basin by basin

Average maps over every HF low (664 in the North Atlantic, 559 in the North Pacific; October to April onsets, 2004–05 to 2022–23), on fixed map coordinates. Top row: 48 hours before the low first reaches HF strength; bottom row: at HF onset. Columns: sea-level pressure, 500 hPa height, 250 hPa wind speed. Colour is the difference from the 1990–2019 average for the same day and hour; lines are the average field itself; magenta dots show where each HF low reached HF strength. These are ERA5 maps on a coarse 1.5° grid, descriptive only, not a test. Because the lows start in different places, the average is smeared: it shows the typical background pattern, not a single storm.

Read with care. These are reanalysis maps on a coarse grid, not observations. The anomaly is measured against 1990–2019, so a warming trend adds a little. The 48-hour maps use all HF lows, including those whose track did not yet exist two days earlier. Wind-speed shading is the average speed minus the long-term average speed.

Before formation

The weather pattern in the week before: HF onset against a storm-force-only peak

Each map shows the difference between the average pattern before an HF low and before a low that only reached storm force, averaged over days −10 to −4 before the onset or peak. Red means higher than before a storm-force-only low, blue lower. Dots mark places where the difference is unlikely to be chance, using a correction for running many comparisons at once (neighbouring points are not independent, so read the dots as a guide). The green outline is the box used for the formal tests, and 12 of the 20 tests set out in advance pass. Click any figure on this page to open it at full size.

The typical pattern

The same patterns as actual maps rather than differences: 500 hPa height contours with 250 hPa wind in colour, and sea-level pressure, for HF onsets (top) and storm-force peaks (bottom).

The two groups differ in age and in where they sit (HF onsets are on average 2.6° of latitude further south than storm-force peaks in the Atlantic and 5.7° in the Pacific), and storms in the same week share an environment. For the statistics, the independent sample is the 22 seasons, not the number of storms.

At the storm

Inside the storm: wind, pressure and moisture maps

Average maps of 400 HF storms and 400 storm-force-only storms per basin, matched by month and turned so the direction of motion points the same way (right of motion is down). Columns: HF onset, HF peak, storm-force peak, and the two differences. Rows: 10 m gust, 10 m wind speed, sea-level pressure, 2 m dewpoint. The gust and wind differences are partly built in, because HF is defined by the gust.

Moisture caveat. HF storms look moister within about 270 nm, but the dewpoint is the raw value, not adjusted for latitude, and HF onsets sit further south (2.6° Atlantic, 5.7° Pacific). Do not read the dewpoint panels as moisture causing HF. 23 of the 28 structure tests set out in advance pass. ERA5 under-resolves the strongest winds, so the areas are biased low; shapes and HF-minus-storm-force differences are more trustworthy than absolute values.

Around every HF low

Storm-centred upper-air maps of all HF lows

Every HF low from October to April, 2004 to 9 January 2023 (664 Atlantic, 559 Pacific), averaged around the storm centre and turned so the direction of motion points right. Columns: 48, 24 and 12 hours before onset, onset and peak. Top row: the average field; bottom row: the difference from the 1990–2019 average. This is a descriptive average of ERA5 on a coarse 1.5° grid, not a test.

Read with care. These are reanalysis fields on a coarse 1.5° grid, not observations. The 250 hPa wind maps show the average wind speed only, not a difference from normal. The storm’s own circulation has not been removed, so the wind pattern near the centre (the weaker wind at the centre and part of the shift of the jet to behind the storm) is partly the storm itself, not the jet. An average smears jet streaks and troughs, so it cannot show entrance and exit regions, trough tilt or trough position. The 48-hour columns rest on the 151 Atlantic and 92 Pacific storms that already had a track then.

Checking notes

An earlier version had a second wind row shaded against the long-term mean wind, which reads too high almost everywhere; that row was removed. Wind speed is the grid-point speed interpolated to the storm box. A fresh agent recomputed every number quoted here from the raw fields; the resampling ranges and the figure pixels were not rechecked. The 250 hPa divergence maps and the north-up views are in the repository under research/era5/hf_env_composites.
